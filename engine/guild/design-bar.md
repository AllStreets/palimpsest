# The design bar

This is the bar the design check holds every interface change to. Score how well the screens, or the change itself when there is nothing to see, meet it, from 0 (it ignores the bar) to 10 (a careful designer would ship it as it stands). A change that keeps a screen as good as it was is not a step down; a change that adds the things below is.

Text is written in sentence case. Headings, buttons, labels and menu items capitalise the first word and proper nouns only. Nothing shouts in capitals, and there are no small capitalised eyebrow labels sitting above headings to announce what the heading already says.

Structure comes from hairlines and space, not from boxes. A thin rule between sections, a quiet border under a header row, generous margins: yes. Every block wrapped in its own bordered, shadowed, rounded card: no. When content is grouped, the grouping should read from alignment and spacing first.

No glow, no neon, no gradients standing in for meaning. No drop shadows that make text or panels float for decoration, no saturated accent colour spread across every control, no dark mode that is just black with bright cyan lines. Colour is used sparingly and means something: a state, a warning, a link.

No grids of identical cards. A page that repeats the same tile with an icon, a bold two-word title and a line of filler text six or nine times reads as a template. Lists of things should look like the things they are, and should differ where the things differ.

Real content, not placeholders. Real names, real numbers, real sentences that say what the screen is for. Lorem ipsum, "Feature one", stock icons standing in for meaning and invented marketing copy all count against the change.

Controls are made for this product. Buttons, inputs, toggles and menus should look like they belong to the rest of the interface, not like a default kit dropped in. A button is as quiet as its job allows; one primary action per view at most.

Tabular data goes in tables. Rows and columns of comparable values belong in a real table with aligned numbers, right-aligned where they are quantities, with a header row, not in a stack of cards or a list of label and value pairs.

Typography comes from the project's own fonts. Use the faces, sizes and weights the project already defines. A change that brings in a generic web font, mixes several families, or sets body text in a display face moves away from the bar. Line length stays readable and the type scale stays consistent with the rest of the product.

When judging before and after screenshots, compare the two. A change that makes a screen calmer, clearer and more like the rest of the product scores higher than the screen before it; one that adds noise, boxes, glow or filler scores lower. When there are no screenshots, judge the change itself: its markup, its styles and its copy, against the same bar, and say that you saw text only.
